Online Appendix

Beliefs in Financial Dialogue (preliminary draft)
1. Data sources 2. Processed data 3. Other data 4. The macro-finance sample 5. Scoring claims with language models

This appendix describes a dataset of interviews on business television. It holds 864,292 question-and-answer exchanges, or Q&As, from interviews on CNBC and Bloomberg Television and on Bloomberg's podcasts, from January 2012 to June 2026. Each Q&A keeps the host's question and the guest's answer as broadcast, the guest's identity, employer and title where they could be established, and the topics of the exchange. Sections 1 to 3 describe the full data. Sections 4 and 5 describe the use of a subset of the data on macro and market topics.

1. Data sources

We collect the closed captions of business-television programs on CNBC and Bloomberg Television from the television news archive of the Internet Archive, and add episodes of Bloomberg's podcasts. The data run from January 2012 to June 2026, because the archive is thin in the earlier years and missing 2011. They cover 16 television programs, nine on CNBC and seven on Bloomberg, and 40,852 episodes with at least one interview. From 2013 to 2025, most hours of the trading day are covered by the two programs (Figure 1).

The vertical axis gives the hour of the day (US Eastern) and the horizontal axis the year. Shading marks how many programs in the data cover each hour of each year: white is not covered, light gray one program, dark gray two.

2. Processed data

We turn the transcripts into a dataset with one row per question-and-answer exchange, or Q&A. After each of the steps below, the output was checked both by human annotators and, at a larger scale, by frontier language models.

From transcripts to interviews

We process each transcript in two steps with OpenAI's gpt-5-mini (version gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07). In the first step the model finds each interview in a transcript and records each guest's name, employer and title as the host introduces them. We favored precision over recall, so the model is told to pass over exchanges among hosts, reports by the network's correspondents and short exchanges. Annotators (humans) examined a sample of interviews (1,035) and for the main shows we find around 97-98% to be true interviews while 2-3% are a mix of exchanges with correspondents. On the largest programs the share is 99% for Squawk Box and 98% for Bloomberg Surveillance. Further steps below in which we extract Q&As (which returns empty field when no substantive Q&A takes place) and, in a later step exclude interviews by media affiliations, addresses this noise substantially.

From interviews to Q&As

In the second step the model reads one interview at a time and divides it into Q&As, leaving out greetings, banter and transitions. A Q&A has to have a substantive economics or finance or politics subject to be allowed. The model assigns each Q&A a primary topic and, where applicable, a secondary topic from a fixed list of 34 topics, together with the main geography, keywords, and one-sentence summaries of the question and the answer. The extraction was done with gpt-5-mini under supervision of Claude Opus: Opus samples Q&As for quality, including interviews which returns either no-Q&As or above 10. For these cases it examines them and re-sends in case of errors and provides a report that is examined by us. Such errors were rare. Annotators then examined the Q&As extracted from 100 interviews (423 Q&As) to inspect whether the Q&A extraction was reasonable, and whether the topic assigned was accurate. The rating was "good", "OK", and "bad". Less than 1% of the Q&As were rated bad on the extraction, and 6.4% were rated bad on one of the two topics assigned to them. The topics rated bad usually concern subjects that are unusual for these programs and do not fit any of our categories well. The downstream analysis (for the macro project) includes another step that further reduces the noise (see Section 5).

Guests, employers and titles

The repeated appearances of the same guests, together with information found online through web searches and the Wikidata API, and the Internet Archive's video frames read with Google's vision model gemini-2.5-flash, let us resolve a large share of the guests. We take a guest as resolved when the guest has a full first and last name and the appearances under that name, with consistent affiliations, are merged into one identity. The manual resolution quality check focused first on the macro-finance sample (Section 4). Where an employer has a Wikidata entry, we record its Wikidata ID. For publicly listed companies we add the Compustat company identifier (GVKEY), which links the employer to company data such as its industry. Guest resolution for the full data remains work in progress (we are currently implementing improvements to this process).

3. Other data

Wall Street Journal articles

To compare print with television, we use 105,127 Wall Street Journal articles from ProQuest TDM Studio, published in sections A and B over about the same period, that are longer than 800 words and contain at least one term from three keyword groups covering equity markets, monetary policy and macroeconomic conditions. OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini gives their paragraphs a topic from the same list of 34 topics as the television Q&As.

Polls data

We complement the data with forecast surveys from individual contributors: the Reuters polls (companies) and the Wall Street Journal Economic Forecasting Survey (economists).

5. Scoring claims with language models

From the Q&As of the macro-finance sample (Section 4), a language model extracts claims. A claim restates one subjective judgment that the guest makes in the answer: an evaluative, predictive or normative statement, including the guest's interpretation of data but not factual citations. DeepSeek's deepseek-chat extracted 1,087,016 claims from the 357,157 Q&As. We select DeepSeek following a manual comparison with gpt-5-mini in this task. Predictions, including verbal ones, are then scored against the consensus of the time, and the model records how much the guest hedges. Section 5.1 of the paper describes the scoring.
